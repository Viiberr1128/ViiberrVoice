# Specificity that earns its place

Details should help the reader understand an actual argument. More words do not turn an invented event into evidence.

**Unsupported opening:** “Someone asks for help in a neighborhood group. Within minutes, a stranger tells them to send a private message.”

This sounds like a witnessed event, but no source establishes who asked, who replied, what was said, or whether it happened at all. The timing and the word “stranger” suggest danger without showing it. Adding a fictional name and more dialogue would make the account more elaborate, not more trustworthy.

**Direct argument, with no scene invented:** “Moving a public conversation into private messages can make it harder for other group members to see what is being offered or challenged.”

That is a reasoned possibility, not a claim that any particular person behaved badly. It needs further evidence before asserting that harm occurred or was widespread.

**Sourced illustration:** Imagine the user supplies this account: “On Tuesday, I asked the group for a wheelchair ramp recommendation. One member replied publicly with the name of a local builder; another offered to share a phone number privately.” The writer can use those details with the user's permission: “When I asked for a ramp recommendation, one neighbor named a builder in the comments. Another offered a phone number privately. The public reply let others see the recommendation; the private offer kept the number out of the thread.”

This illustration is explicitly supplied for the exercise. It establishes no actual event. The distinction between public and private replies is observable; it does not establish bad intent or a harmful outcome.

For a real assignment, use the user's evidence and preserve uncertainty. When no evidence supports a scene, make the argument directly or ask for the missing facts instead of creating a more vivid story.
